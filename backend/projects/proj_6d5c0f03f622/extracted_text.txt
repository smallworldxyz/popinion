Thailand’s 2025 Polycrisis: A Structural 
Analysis of Political Dissolution, 
Geopolitical Conflict, and Economic 
Stagnation 
Executive Summary 
As of December 2025, the Kingdom of Thailand is navigating a convergence of systemic crises 
that threatens to unravel decades of development and stability. This "polycrisis"—a term 
denoting the entanglement of simultaneous, mutually reinforcing catastrophic 
events—manifests across three primary dimensions: a collapsing political order precipitated 
by judicial intervention, a two-front geopolitical conflict involving active warfare, and a 
structural economic decline exacerbated by policy failures and demographic attrition. 
The dissolution of Parliament on December 12, 2025, has plunged the nation into a volatile 
interim period ahead of general elections scheduled for February 8, 2026. This political 
vacuum occurs against the backdrop of the most severe border hostilities in a generation: a 
reignited war with Cambodia on the eastern frontier and a historic rupture in diplomatic 
relations with Myanmar to the west. These conflicts have paralyzed border trade, severed 
labor supply chains, and invited competing interventions from the United States and China, 
transforming Thailand into a theater of great power rivalry. 
Economically, the nation faces a "lost decade." GDP growth has decelerated to 1.2% in the 
third quarter of 2025, with forecasts for 2026 dipping to a five-year low of roughly 1.5%. The 
failure of the flagship "Digital Wallet" stimulus scheme, combined with household debt levels 
approaching 90% of GDP, has suffocated domestic consumption. Simultaneously, the country 
is hurtling toward a "super-aged" society status without having achieved high-income 
stability, a demographic cliff that is currently exacerbating a critical labor shortage. 
This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the state of Thailand in December 2025. It 
synthesizes data on political maneuvering, military engagements, macroeconomic indicators, 
and social fragility to offer a comprehensive assessment of a nation at a pivotal historical 
juncture. 
Part I: The Political Interregnum and the Crisis of 
Legitimacy 
The political landscape of Thailand in late 2025 is defined by the disintegration of the coalition 
government formed in 2023 and the reassertion of the conservative establishment's power 


through judicial mechanisms. The fragile détente between the Shinawatra political dynasty 
and the traditional elite has shattered, giving way to a new, harder-edged authoritarianism 
under the banner of the Bhumjaithai Party (BJT). 
1.1 The Judicial Decapitation of the Executive 
The proximate cause of the current political vacuum was the Constitutional Court's ruling on 
August 29, 2025, which removed Prime Minister Paetongtarn Shinawatra from office.1 This 
dismissal was not merely a procedural correction but a significant expansion of judicial 
authority over foreign policy and executive conduct. 
The court's decision hinged on a leaked teleconference between Paetongtarn and former 
Cambodian Prime Minister Hun Sen. During the call, which took place in June 2025 amidst 
rising border tensions, Paetongtarn referred to the Cambodian strongman as "uncle"—a 
familial honorific common in Thai culture—and discussed reopening the border to alleviate 
domestic economic pressure. She notably referred to General Boonsin Padklang, commander 
of the 2nd Army Area, as part of the "opposition," signaling a dangerous rift between the 
civilian government and the military.2 
The Constitutional Court interpreted these interactions as a violation of ethical standards, 
ruling that Paetongtarn’s conduct diminished "national prestige" and compromised the 
"national interest." The ruling set a profound and restrictive precedent: 
●​ Criminalization of Diplomacy: By framing conciliatory dialogue during a conflict as 
ethical misconduct, the court has effectively constrained future leaders from utilizing 
back-channel diplomacy. The ruling implies that any de-escalation that can be 
construed as a concession to an adversary is grounds for removal, forcing subsequent 
administrations into hawkish postures to survive judicial scrutiny.2 
●​ The "Prestige" Standard: The court established that the "mere possibility of public 
suspicion" regarding a leader's defense of sovereignty is sufficient for dismissal, 
creating a vague and weaponizable standard that elevates public sentiment (as 
interpreted by the judiciary) above electoral mandates.2 
This ruling marked the second removal of a Pheu Thai Prime Minister in two years, following 
Srettha Thavisin in 2024, effectively decapitating the party’s leadership structure and leaving 
it ideologically adrift.3 
1.2 The Rise of the "Blue" Establishment: Anutin Charnvirakul 
Into this vacuum stepped Anutin Charnvirakul, leader of the Bhumjaithai Party (BJT). Initially 
serving as a caretaker, Anutin was formally endorsed by a coalition including the People’s 
Party in September 2025, only to dissolve the House on December 12, 2025, citing the need 
for a fresh mandate to address the "poly-crisis".1 
The ascendancy of the "Blue" faction (associated with the BJT and conservative elites) 
represents a shift from the military-led governments of the 2014-2023 era to a more astute, 
electoral authoritarianism. Anutin has skillfully leveraged the ongoing border conflict to 
cultivate a nationalist persona, positioning himself as the "defender of the realm." His rhetoric 
explicitly rejects the trade-off between lives and votes, yet his administration’s focus on the 


war has conveniently marginalized opposition narratives.5 
Strategic Consolidation: 
●​ Cabinet Continuity: To reassure the business sector amidst the turmoil, Anutin has 
pledged to retain key technocrats in a future administration, specifically naming Finance 
Minister Ekniti Nitithanprapas and Foreign Minister Sihasak Phuangketkeow. This signals 
a desire to maintain the "economic technocracy" while pursuing a nationalist security 
agenda.4 
●​ The "War for Votes" Accusation: Critics argue that the BJT is exploiting the conflict 
with Cambodia to stir nationalist fervor, a tactic historically effective in Thai politics. 
Anutin has vigorously denied these claims, stating, "I do not have the courage to trade 
the lives of our soldiers or citizens for votes," but the timing of the escalation and the 
subsequent dissolution of parliament remains suspicious to observers.6 
1.3 The People’s Party and the Paralysis of Reform 
The People’s Party (PP), the successor to the dissolved Move Forward Party, finds itself in a 
precarious position. Despite holding the largest bloc of seats (143) prior to dissolution, the 
party is besieged by legal warfare and strategic dilemmas.3 
Barriers to Power: 
●​ Legal Attrition: The party is facing a relentless campaign of "lawfare." For instance, 
former MP Jirat Thongsuwan was sentenced to two years in prison for conscription 
evasion, a case resurrected to delegitimize the party's platform of military reform.7 
●​ The Nationalism Trap: The border conflict has placed the PP in a bind. If they advocate 
for peace, they risk being branded as unpatriotic or weak on sovereignty—a narrative 
the BJT is eager to promote. If they support the military action, they alienate their 
progressive base which opposes military dominance. Party leader Natthaphong 
Ruengpanyawut has attempted to thread this needle by calling for a "three-front 
strategy" focusing on suppressing scam centers rather than military confrontation, but 
this nuanced message struggles to compete with the visceral imagery of border 
clashes.8 
●​ Internal Discord: The party’s momentum has stalled, evidenced by Leader 
Natthaphong’s recent public apology for decision-making errors, suggesting internal 
friction regarding how to navigate the hostility of the conservative establishment.9 
1.4 The February 2026 Election Landscape 
The general election scheduled for February 8, 2026, is poised to be a referendum on the 
nation’s direction: continued conservative management under the guise of national security, 
or a return to the turbulent path of structural reform.3

Part II: The Geopolitical Siege – The Ring of Fire 
Thailand’s traditional foreign policy of "bamboo diplomacy"—bending with the wind to 
maintain equilibrium—has catastrophically failed. The nation is now encircled by active hostile 
fronts on both its east and west, a situation described by analysts as a "Ring of Fire." 
2.1 The Thailand-Cambodia Border War 
The conflict on the eastern border has escalated from skirmishes into a limited war, 
characterized by heavy artillery exchanges, airstrikes, and significant displacement of 
civilians. 
2.1.1 Timeline of Escalation 
The deterioration of relations followed a rapid, violent trajectory throughout 2025: 
●​ July 24–28, 2025 (The "Five-Day War"): Hostilities erupted near the Ta Muen Thom 
and Preah Vihear temples. The fighting involved rocket artillery and F-16 airstrikes, 
resulting in at least 48 fatalities and the displacement of 300,000 civilians. This phase 
concluded with a fragile ceasefire mediated by Malaysia.12 
●​ October 26, 2025 (The Kuala Lumpur Peace Accord): In a high-profile diplomatic 
event, a peace deal was signed in Kuala Lumpur, witnessed by U.S. President Donald 
Trump and Malaysian Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim. The accord mandated a phased 


withdrawal of heavy weaponry.15 
●​ November 2025 (The Breakdown): The truce fractured after Thai soldiers were 
maimed by landmines near the border. Thailand accused Cambodia of laying fresh 
mines in violation of the accord; Cambodia insisted they were remnants of past wars. 
This incident served as the casus belli for renewed hostility.16 
●​ December 7–8, 2025 (Resumption of Full-Scale War): Heavy fighting resumed, with 
Thailand declaring "Peace is over." This phase has seen aerial bombardments and the 
expansion of conflict zones, displacing a combined total of over 750,000 people.12 
2.1.2 Drivers of Conflict 
The war is driven by a nexus of historical grievance and modern illicit economics: 
●​ The Preah Vihear Legacy: The century-old dispute over colonial maps drawn by 
France remains the emotional core of the conflict. The "Dangrek escarpment" line 
creates ambiguous sovereignty over ancient temples, which serves as a potent rallying 
cry for nationalists in both Bangkok and Phnom Penh.17 
●​ The Scam Center Proxy War: A modern driver is the transnational crime syndicates 
operating in Cambodia. The Thai government perceives these scam centers, often 
located in border towns like Poipet, as threats to its citizens' financial security. 
Thailand’s crackdown on these centers—which allegedly funnel profits to Cambodian 
elites—has antagonized the Hun Manet administration.18 
●​ Deterrence Failure: Analysts argue that Thailand faces a "deterrence failure." Despite 
superior military hardware, Thailand is constrained by the need to keep the war limited 
to avoid regional destabilization, while Cambodia, with less to lose, acts with greater 
assertiveness. This "resolve gap" is exacerbated by the Thai elite's internal 
fragmentation.20 
2.1.3 Internationalization of the Conflict 
The border war has become a proxy theater for US-China competition: 
●​ United States: President Trump’s personal involvement in the October accord was a 
strategic move to assert US relevance. However, his subsequent tweet calling a 
landmine incident an "accident" was viewed in Bangkok as undermining Thai security 
concerns, while Phnom Penh embraced it, even proposing him for a Nobel Peace Prize.11 
●​ China: Beijing has maintained a "low-profile" but decisive role, mediating through 
Foreign Minister Wang Yi. China’s deep economic leverage over Cambodia allows it to 
play the role of ultimate arbiter, a position the US has failed to dislodge.7 
2.2 The Western Front: Diplomatic Rupture with Myanmar 
Simultaneous to the eastern war, Thailand’s relationship with Myanmar has collapsed, creating 
a two-front security crisis. 
●​ Diplomatic Severance: On December 8, 2025, the Myanmar junta declared a formal 
break in diplomatic relations with Thailand, closing its embassy and consulates.24 This 
historic rupture appears to be a reaction to Thailand’s perceived tolerance of anti-junta 


elements and disputes over border trade revenues. 
●​ Economic & Security Fallout: The break has nullified regional trade agreements and 
closed the vital Mae Sot-Myawaddy trade corridor. Security skirmishes have surged 
along the western border, forcing the Thai military to split its resources between two 
active fronts.24 
●​ Refugee Labor Pivot: In a desperate and controversial move to address labor 
shortages caused by the Cambodian conflict, the Thai cabinet approved work permits 
for 40,000 Myanmar refugees residing in border camps. This policy shift, while 
economically necessary, risks further antagonizing the Myanmar junta and complicating 
the security landscape.25 
Part III: The Economic Asphyxiation 
Thailand’s economy in December 2025 is characterizing by stagnation, structural decay, and 
the immediate shocks of war. The hope for a post-pandemic recovery has evaporated, 
replaced by the reality of a "lost decade" trajectory. 
3.1 Macroeconomic Stagnation 
The latest data from the National Economic and Social Development Council (NESDC) paints a 
grim picture of an economy losing momentum. 
●​ GDP Deceleration: The economy expanded by only 1.2% in the third quarter of 2025, a 
sharp deceleration from the 2.8% recorded in the second quarter. On a 
quarter-on-quarter basis, the economy actually contracted by 0.6%.27 
●​ Forecast Downgrades: The Bank of Thailand and private sector analysts have 
downgraded 2026 growth forecasts to between 1.5% and 1.8%, the lowest potential 
growth rate in five years.28 This suggests that Thailand is firmly stuck in the 
"middle-income trap," unable to compete with low-cost neighbors like Vietnam or 
high-tech economies like South Korea. 
3.2 The Collapse of the Digital Wallet Scheme 
The flagship economic policy of the Pheu Thai-led coalition—the 10,000 baht "Digital Wallet" 
handout—has effectively collapsed, serving as a case study in failed populist economics. 
●​ Policy Failure: Originally budgeted at 500 billion baht to stimulate grassroots 
consumption, the scheme faced immense implementation hurdles. By late 2025, the 
budget was scaled back to 450 billion baht, and eventually, the project was "effectively 
shelved." The government began reallocating the remaining 157 billion baht to other 
emergency measures, such as flood relief and tourism support.30 
●​ No Multiplier Effect: The initial limited distribution failed to produce the anticipated 
economic multiplier. Economists noted that one-off cash injections could not overcome 
the structural headwinds of high debt and weak export demand. The failure of this 
policy has severely damaged the credibility of the Pheu Thai party.30 


3.3 The Household Debt Suffocation 
Consumption, the traditional engine of the Thai economy, is being strangled by one of the 
highest household debt ratios in Asia. 
●​ Debt Overhang: Household debt stands at approximately 87-89% of GDP as of late 
2025.33 The sheer volume of debt service is diverting income away from consumption. 
●​ The Negative Wealth Effect: Asset prices, particularly in real estate and automobiles, 
are stagnating. This negative "wealth effect" has caused consumer confidence to 
plummet to 44.7 in Q3 2025. With hire-purchase businesses for vehicles facing stricter 
regulations starting December 2025, access to credit is tightening further, deepening 
the slump in durable goods consumption.27 
3.4 The Triple Trade Shock 
Thailand’s export-dependent economy is being battered by a "triple shock" of war, 
protectionism, and currency volatility. 
●​ Border Trade Paralysis: The conflicts with Cambodia and Myanmar have decimated 
cross-border commerce. The closure of the Klong Luek–Poipet crossing alone is 
estimated to cost the economy tens of billions of baht annually. Trade with Myanmar has 
similarly plummeted following the diplomatic rupture.12 
●​ Trat Province Case Study: In just nine days of fighting in December, Trat province 
reported economic losses exceeding 1 billion baht ($32 million), illustrating the acute 
localized impact of the war.38 
●​ U.S. Tariffs: The imposition of tariffs by the U.S. administration on goods from the 
region has hit Thai supply chains. While some front-loading of orders occurred in 2025 
to beat the tariffs, a "payback effect" is expected in 2026, leading to a potential export 
contraction of up to 3.1% in the worst-case scenario.29 
3.5 Tourism: A Broken Engine 
Tourism, which accounts for roughly 20% of GDP, is faltering under the weight of geopolitical 
instability. 
●​ Collapse in High-Value Zones: The border war has devastated tourism in the eastern 
provinces. High-value destinations like Koh Kood have seen occupancy rates drop to 
20% during the peak season due to security fears.38 
●​ Competitiveness Crisis: Industry associations warn that Thailand is becoming 
uncompetitive compared to Vietnam and Japan due to the strengthening Baht and 
safety concerns. The target of 39 million arrivals for 2026 is viewed with extreme 
skepticism given the active conflict zones and travel advisories issued by Western 
nations.40 
Part IV: The Social and Demographic Cliff 


Beneath the headlines of war and politics, Thailand is undergoing a slow-motion social 
collapse driven by irreversible demographic trends and labor market dysfunctions. 
4.1 The "Super-Aged" Society 
Thailand is aging before it has become wealthy, a demographic trajectory that threatens fiscal 
sustainability. 
●​ Demographic Collapse: Thailand officially became a "fully aged society" in 2024 and is 
hurtling toward "super-aged" status by 2036. In 2024, births fell to a record low of 
approximately 460,000, while the population of those aged 60+ is projected to reach 
31.1% by 2040.43 
●​ Fiscal Strain: The shrinking workforce means a smaller tax base must support a rapidly 
growing elderly population. The state faces a looming "care crisis," with a projected 
shortage of 250,000 caregivers by 2037. The current budget is insufficient to provide 
adequate welfare, threatening a humanitarian crisis among the elderly poor.44 
4.2 The Labor Market Crisis and Migrant Exodus 
Thailand’s economic model relies heavily on low-cost migrant labor from neighboring 
countries. The current geopolitical crisis has severed this lifeline. 
●​ The Great Exodus: The border war with Cambodia caused an estimated 780,000 to 1 
million Cambodian workers to flee Thailand and return home due to safety fears and 
discrimination.12 
●​ Sectoral Paralysis: This exodus has triggered acute labor shortages in construction, 
agriculture, and fisheries—sectors that Thais are unwilling to work in. Business leaders 
in border provinces report that labor costs have doubled, further eroding 
competitiveness.38 
●​ The Myanmar Refugee Pivot: In a desperate attempt to plug the labor gap, the Thai 
cabinet approved work permits for 40,000 Myanmar refugees in border camps. While 
pragmatically necessary, this move is politically fraught given the tensions with the 
Myanmar junta.25 
4.3 Education and Inequality 
The quality of human capital is insufficient to power the government's "Thailand 4.0" 
ambition. 
●​ Educational Failure: PISA scores indicate a sharp decline in reading and math 
proficiency. A staggering 47% of 15-year-olds in rural village schools are classified as 
"functionally illiterate".48 
●​ Skills Mismatch: There is a critical shortage of skilled labor for the digital economy. The 
education system continues to produce graduates with degrees that do not match 
market needs, exacerbating the middle-income trap and leaving the youth unemployed 
or underemployed.49 


Part V: The Environmental Emergency – PM2.5 and 
Smoke 
As December 2025 progresses, the seasonal accumulation of PM2.5 particulate matter is 
reaching hazardous levels, adding a layer of immediate physical danger to the populace. 
5.1 The Smog Season of 2025/2026 
The PM2.5 levels are forecast to peak between November 2025 and May 2026, threatening a 
repeat of the 2023 crisis where over 10 million people sought medical treatment for 
pollution-related illnesses. 
●​ Sources of Pollution: The haze is driven by a combination of agricultural burning 
(particularly sugarcane), vehicular emissions, and stagnant weather patterns.51 
●​ War-Induced Haze: A new variable in 2025 is the smoke from the border conflict. 
Artillery shelling and military maneuvers along the Cambodian border have ignited 
forest fires, contributing to the transboundary haze affecting eastern Thailand and 
Bangkok.53 
5.2 Political Weaponization of Air 
The pollution crisis has become a central theme in domestic politics. 
●​ Policy Response: The government has launched the "Green List Plus" project to 
incentivize business compliance and has prepared measures like "dust-free classrooms" 
and voluntary work-from-home orders.55 
●​ Public Dissatisfaction: Opposition parties have seized on the smog as a symbol of 
state failure. The delay in passing the Clean Air Act and the inability to control 
agricultural burning are cited as evidence of the government's prioritization of 
corporate interests over public health. The visceral experience of "choking" on bad air 
resonates deeply with the urban middle class, potentially mobilizing them against the 
government in the upcoming election.51 
Conclusion: Scenarios for 2026 
As Thailand enters 2026, it stands on a precipice. The "polycrisis" is not merely a collection of 
isolated challenges but a systemic dysfunction where each crisis amplifies the others. The 
border wars drain the fiscal resources needed for economic stimulus. The economic 
stagnation fuels the nationalist populism that drives the border wars. The political 
fragmentation prevents the long-term planning required to address the demographic and 
environmental emergencies. 
Key Watchpoints for Q1 2026: 
1.​ The February 8 Election: Will the Bhumjaithai Party secure a mandate to continue its 
hardline policies, or will a fragmented parliament lead to a weak coalition susceptible to 
military intervention? The polling data showing BJT at only 9.9% suggests a high risk of a 


hung parliament or a coalition of necessity that lacks stability.4 
2.​ Conflict Trajectory: Will the breakdown of the Kuala Lumpur Peace Accord lead to a 
wider war involving heavy mechanized units, or can international pressure (US/China) 
force a freeze? The involvement of global powers raises the stakes significantly. 
3.​ Economic Resilience: Will the export sector collapse under the weight of US tariffs and 
the Baht's volatility? If exports contract by the forecasted 3.1%, Thailand could enter a 
technical recession. 
Thailand's stability in 2026 depends on whether its leadership can transition from crisis 
exploitation—using war for votes—to crisis management. The current trajectory suggests a 
deepening of authoritarian nationalism as a substitute for delivering economic prosperity, a 
strategy that risks isolating Thailand within ASEAN and severing the global supply chains upon 
which its fading economic model depends.
